What is SAR, in Plain Words?

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active microwave satellite sensor that functions like an orbital flashlight. Unlike optical satellites (such as Sentinel-2 or Google Earth) which require clear skies and sunlight, SAR emits its own radar beams and measures the energy that bounces back from the Earth's surface—called backscatter (σ0).

Because C-band microwaves (at 5.5 cm wavelength) pass directly through rain clouds and monsoon haze, SAR provides unbroken observations throughout the Kharif season when optical sensors are blinded by cloud cover. As crops grow—transitioning from tilled soil, through rapid leaf emergence, dense canopy flowering, to dry harvest—their radar backscatter creates an unmistakable phenological fingerprint that allows machine learning algorithms to accurately map crop types.

Study Area & Cropping Calendar

The study covers the Baramati Agricultural Belt in Pune District, Maharashtra (74.50°E – 74.65°E, 18.10°N – 18.25°N; ~260 km2). Sustained by the Nira Left Bank Canal and groundwater, this semi-arid Deccan plateau zone combines perennial cash crops with rainfed Kharif cereals and pulses.
